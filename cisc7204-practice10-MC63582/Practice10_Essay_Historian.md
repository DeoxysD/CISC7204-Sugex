## Essay body (paste into the forum message)

**Historical review of:** E. Saccenti, “A gentle introduction to principal component analysis using tea-pots, dinosaurs, and pizza,” *Teaching Statistics* 46 (2024): 38–52. DOI: 10.1111/test.12363

**Where this paper sits.** Viewed historically, Saccenti’s article is not a foundational PCA paper; it is a 2024 *teaching* synthesis that re-packages a century-old method for modern data-literacy classrooms. Its intellectual lineage begins with classical multivariate geometry, continues through mid-20th-century educational and psychometric uses of components, and arrives in contemporary statistics education that prioritizes visual intuition and software practice over theorem-first linear algebra. The paper’s distinctive move—introduce PCA *from visualization and rotation*, then arrive at dimensionality reduction—places it in the pedagogy-of-visualization stream more than in the algorithmic PCA literature. It explicitly cites Pearson (1901) and Hotelling (1933) as origin points, notes their formulations are equivalent but not identical in concept, and situates PCA among tools needed for high-dimensional “modern data,” echoing calls to teach methods suited to today’s multivariate reality rather than only classical univariate recipes.

**Prior work 1 — Pearson (1901).** Karl Pearson’s “On lines and planes of closest fit to systems of points in space” (*Philosophical Magazine*, 1901) frames the problem geometrically: find lines/planes that best fit a cloud of points by minimizing squared distances. This is the geometric ancestor of PCA as fitting a low-dimensional subspace. Saccenti’s teapot and triceratops demos are pedagogical dramatizations of that same idea—structure is a configuration of points in space, and a good “view” (rotation / subspace) makes the structure recognizable. Influence on the present paper is foundational and conceptual: without Pearson’s closest-fit geometry, the author’s story that “rotation reveals structure” and later “maximum-variance directions define PCs” would lack its classical anchor. The 2024 article does not extend Pearson’s mathematics; it *translates* the geometric intuition into classroom visuals and trigonometric linear combinations accessible before full matrix algebra.

**Prior work 2 — Hotelling (1933).** Harold Hotelling’s “Analysis of a complex of statistical variables into principal components” (*Journal of Educational Psychology*, 1933) reformulates the problem in the language of statistical variables, variance, and successive uncorrelated components ordered by importance—closer to how software and textbooks present PCA today (scores, variance explained, uncorrelated PCs). Saccenti’s classroom pipeline (variance as information; rotate toward maximum variance; require uncorrelated successive components; then reduce dimensions) inherits Hotelling’s statistical framing more directly than Pearson’s pure geometry. Influence is therefore high on *definition and interpretation*: loadings as weights, variance explained as the success metric, and PCs as new variables replacing correlated originals. The pizza and gene-expression case studies are applied descendants of Hotelling’s educational-psychometric ambition to summarize many correlated measurements with few components.

**How influential these priors are to this work.** Extremely influential as *intellectual parents*, lightly influential as *methodological novelty*. Saccenti relies on Pearson–Hotelling for legitimacy and correct definitions, then adds instructional design: memorable 2-D/3-D objects, pizza chemistry for score/loading literacy, a failure case (psoriasis genes) to teach when correlation structure is absent, Matlab/`pca` practice, and an optional linear-algebra appendix. Relative to the priors, originality lies in curriculum sequencing and visualization-first rhetoric, not in a new estimator. A historian of visualization would also note continuity with later popular visual explainers of PCA (variance axes, “flattening” correlated variables): Saccenti formalizes that tradition for *Teaching Statistics* with reproducible data/code. In short, Pearson supplies the geometric soul, Hotelling the statistical grammar; Saccenti supplies a 21st-century visual syllabus that prepares students to use PCA as a data-visualization instrument rather than a mysterious preprocessing step.

**Problem, contributions, strengths, limits (historian’s wrap).** The enduring problem is teaching multivariate structure without drowning beginners in eigenanalysis. Contribution: a tested lecture+practical pathway from visual rotation to PCA-as-reduction. Strength: memorable examples and honest “PCA not helpful” discussion. Limits: brief evaluation; linear/Pearson scope; life-science flavor. Methods: trigonometry + variance/correlation demos; Matlab PCA; public datasets/GitLab codes. Running PCA historically “looks like”: center/standardize → extract ordered uncorrelated components → plot leading scores/loadings—software changes, the Pearson–Hotelling goal does not.
