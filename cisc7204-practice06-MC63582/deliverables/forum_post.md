My Mislaeding Visual Identified

The visual I located is a dual-axis line chart created by Americans United for Life and displayed by Rep. Jason Chaffetz during a U.S. House Oversight hearing on 29 September 2015. The graphic compares Planned Parenthood cancer screening and prevention services with abortions between 2006 and 2013. Its headline message is that abortions went up while “life-saving procedures” went down. The endpoint numbers printed on the chart are real: about 2,007,371 cancer-related services and 289,750 abortions in 2006, versus 935,573 and 327,000 in 2013 (Lee, 2015; Qiu, 2015). The picture those numbers are forced into is not honest.

I attach two images. `misleading_visual.png` is the published side-by-side from FiveThirtyEight (Chalabi, 2015): the encoder’s original graphic form on the left, and the same endpoint values redrawn on one common y-axis on the right. `annotated_findings.png` marks Cairo’s mechanisms on that comparison and restores service categories that the original chart omitted.

Annotations of findings

1. The decoder’s first reading is controlled by position, not by the tiny labels. Because the abortion line is drawn from the bottom-left to the top-right, and the cancer line is drawn from the top-left toward the middle-right, the arrows cross. A crossing line chart usually means one quantity has overtaken another. Here the overtaking is an artifact of scale. 327,000 is still far smaller than 935,573.

2. The original chart does not label a y-axis. Dual-axis charts are already easy to abuse; an unlabeled dual-axis chart removes the last cue that the two series are not sharing a ruler. As Cairo told PolitiFact, “we cannot force the data to adopt a shape we like” (Qiu, 2015).

3. Arrowheads on both lines tell the decoder that the invented slopes will continue after 2013. Two points cannot support that claim.

4. Only two years and only two service types are shown. STI/STD testing rose to about 4.5 million services, contraceptive services remained about 3.6 million, and all non-abortion services together were almost unchanged: 10.29 million in 2006 and 10.26 million in 2013 (Lee, 2015). The decline in Pap smears and related screenings is also partly explained by changed medical guidelines, not by a simple substitution of abortions for care (Qiu, 2015).

Misleading mechanisms (Cairo, 2015)

I read the visual with Cairo’s three strategies: hiding relevant data to highlight what benefits the encoder; displaying too much data to obscure reality; and using graphic forms in inappropriate ways so the data are distorted.

Mechanism 1 — Distorting data through graphic forms (primary). The encoder maps two counts that share the same unit onto two invisible, incompatible vertical scales. Position is no longer proportional to quantity, so the decoder sees abortions “overtake” cancer screenings when the numbers say the opposite. Arrowheads further distort the message by turning two endpoints into an unstoppable future trend. This is exactly Cairo’s third strategy: the graphic form is used so that the picture and the data disagree.

Mechanism 2 — Hiding relevant data (secondary). The encoder shows only the one declining “life-saving” series and only the years that make the contrast look dramatic. Intermediate years, STI/STD work, contraception, and the stable total of non-abortion services are left out. The average-style story (“abortions up, care down”) survives only because the rest of the dataset is hidden. This is Cairo’s first strategy.

Not used — Displaying too much data. The chart is sparse rather than crowded. Cairo’s second strategy does not apply here.

A short encoder–decoder reading: the encoder wants a single memorable crossing; the decoder, who trusts that higher on the page means larger in the world, walks away with the wrong comparison. The ethical problem is the encoding, not the political topic. Cairo’s own verdict on this same chart was that the distortion is “ethically wrong” (Qiu, 2015).
